ON THE DEATH OF THEODOSIUS. 


1. Now severe earthquakes were threatening us, now continual 
rains, and darkness denser than ordinary night was announcing that 
our most merciful Emperor Theodosius was about to leave this 
earth. Even the elements, then, were mourning his death. The 
heavens were veiled in obscurity, the air shuddering in unbroken 
gloom; the earth was shaken by tremors, was filled with floods 
of waters. Why should not the very universe weep because he, the 
prince, was to be forthwith snatched away by death, he by whom 
the hardships of this world were accustomed to be mitigated, since 
by forgiveness he avoided the punishment of crime. 

2. And he indeed, has gone to receive his kingdom, which he 
has not laid aside, but, admitted by right of piety into the taber- 
nacles of Christ, he has exchanged it for the heavenly Jerusalem. 
Now that he has received his place there, he says: “ As we have 
heard, so have we seen in the city of the Lord of hosts, in the 
city of our God, which God hath founded forever.” But he has 
left behind many destitute, as it were, of a father’s protection, 
and above all his sons. But they are not destitute whom he left 
as the heirs of his piety ; they are not destitute for whom he gained 
the grace of Christ and the loyalty of the army, to which he was 
the proof that God cherishes devotion and avenges treachery. 

3. Therefore, recently we lamented the death of this prince, 
and now we are celebrating the fortieth day, with the prince 
Honorius assisting at the holy altar. Because as holy Joseph per- 
formed the burial rites for his father Jacob during forty days, 60 
this son offers due ceremonies for his father Theodosius. And 
because some are accustomed to observe the third and the thirtieth 
day, others the seventh and the fortieth, let us consider what the 
text teaches. Thus when Jacob died, “Joseph commanded,” 
it says, “the servant undertakers to bury him, and the undertakers 
buried Israel, and forty days were completed for him ; for thus the 
days of the funeral rites are reckoned. And Egypt mourned for 
him seventy days.” Accordingly, that solemnity which the text 


prescribes is to be followed. But also in Deuteronomy it is written

that the children of Israel mourned for Moses thirty days, and the 
days of mourning were finished. Each observance, then, has au- 
thority because the necessary duty of filial piety is accomplished. 

4. Therefore Joseph was good, who furnished the model for 
filial devotion, whom his father loved, and to whom he said: “ May 
my God aid thee, and may he bless thee with the blessing of holding 
all things of the earth because of the blessing of the breasts and 
of the womb, blessings of thy mother, and because of the blessings 
of thy father”; Joseph, the good offspring of a devoted father. 
He therefore celebrates the fortieth day of his father Jacob, that 
supplantor, and we celebrate the fortieth day of Theodosius, who, 
after the example of Jacob, supplanted the perfidy of tyrants, who 
put away the idols of the gentiles, for his faith has removed all 
worship of images, has stamped out all their ceremonies. He also 
grieved that the remission of punishment, which he had granted 
to those who had transgressed against him, had come to naught and 
pardon had been denied. But his sons will not refuse what their 
father granted. They will not refuse, even though a certain person 
should attempt to cause a disturbance; for those who pay out what 
he left to individuals, will not be able to refuse what he left for all. 

5. The death of so great a prince had in it nothing more glorious, 
who had already consigned all to his sons; his kingdom, his power, 
the name of Augustus. Nothing, I say, more seemly was reserved 
for him in death than the fact that since the promised mitigation 
of the necessary payment of the grain tax in some cases was delayed, 
his successor has become the heir of these indulgences, so that 
the one who wished to prevent this has made ill-will for himself. 
Nevertheless, the crown of so great a favour has not been taken 
away from Theodosius. Not undeservedly, for if the last wishes 
of private citizens have perpetual stability, even though testaments 
are lacking, how can the will of so great a prince be disregarded? 
Theodosius is also glorious in this, that he did not attest by com- 
mon law; for he had nothing unusual to conceal from his sons to 
whom he had given everything, unless it were to commend them 
to a father who was present. It was necessary for him to provide 
by will concerning all that belonged to him and had been com- 
mitted to his care, so that he might do away with legacies and 
designate all things left in trust. He ordered the law of indul- 






gence to be passed which he left written. What more worthy than 
that this law be the last will of the emperor ? 

6. Therefore so great an emperor has withdrawn from us, but 
he has not wholly withdrawn; for he has left us his children in 
whom we should recognize him, and in whom we see and possess 
him. Let not their age disquiet you. The faith of soldiers is the 
perfection of the age of an emperor; for age is perfect where 
strength is perfect. This acts reciprocally, for the faith of an 
emperor is the strength of his soldiers. 

7. You will recall, I am sure, what triumphs the faith of Theodo- 
sius acquired for you. When, because of local difficulties and the 
hindrance of camp followers, the army line came down to the 
combat a little too slowly and because of your delay in offering 
battle the enemy seemed to insult you, the prince leaped down 
from his horse and advancing alone before the line, said: “ Where 
is the God of Theodosius? ” He spoke thus when already close to 
Christ ; for who could have said this unless one who knew that he 
was close to Christ? By this speech he aroused all, and by his 
example armed all. And surely, he was now somewhat advanced 
in years, but robust in faith. 

8. The faith of Theodosius then, was your victory; let your faith 
be the strength of his sons. Faith, therefore, adds to age. Finally 
Abraham did not consider age when, an old man, he begot a son, 
nor Sara, when she bore a son. It is not astonishing if faith adds 
to age, since it brings the future before one. For what is faith 
except the substance of those things for which we hope? So the 
Scriptures teach us. Therefore if faith is the substance of those 
things for which we hope, how much the more, of those which 
we see? (Good faith of which it is written: “But the just man 
liveth by faith; but if he withdraw himself, he shall not please 
my soul.” 

9. Moreover let us not withdraw ourselves at the expense of our 
souls, but let us cling to faith for our soul’s gain; since in this 
warfare of faith our elders, Abraham, Isaac, and J acob, have known 
the evidence, and thus they have left to us a heritage of faith. 
Abraham was faithful, who not by works but by faith was justified, 
since he believed God; Isaac, who through faith did not fear the 
sword of his father, who was about to strike him; Jacob, who

intent upon the footprints of his father’s faith, while he made 
his journey, saw an army of angels and called it the council of God. 

10. Elsewhere also, that is, in the book of Kings, Eliseus was 
in Samaria, and suddenly an army of Syrians surrounded and set 
upon him. Giezi saw them and said to his master, “O Master, 
what shall we do?” And Eliseus, the prophet, said, *‘ Fear not, 
for there are more with us than with them.” And he prayed that 
the Lord would open the eyes of Giezi. And his eyes were opened 
and he saw the mountain full of horses and chariots around Eliseus. 
And Eliseus prayed that God would strike then with blindness and 
they were struck, and they entered into the city whither they were 
going, seeing not at all.” Surely, you soldiers who have been 
surrounded have heard that where there is perfidy, there is blind- 
ness. Therefore, deservedly was the army of the unbeliever blind. 
But where there is faith there is an army of angels. And so faith 
is good, which frequently has effect among the dead. Finally, our 
adversary and his legions are daily hurled back by the virtue of 
the martyrs. So I think that on this account the strings of the 
cithern are called fides, since, although dead, they give forth sound. 

11. Thus we must strive more and more, lest while engaged in 
this duty of living, we be ungrateful; but let us bestow sedulous 
and paternal affection on the children of the pious prince. Pay 
to his sons what you owe to their father; you owe more to him 
now that he is dead than you owed to him while he was living. 
For if among the children of private citizens the rights of minors 
are not violated without grave crime, how much the more in the 
case of the children of an emperor. 

12. Let it be said in addition, “ Of what an emperor!” Of a 
pious emperor, of a merciful emperor, of a faithful emperor, con- 
cerning whom the Scripture has spoken in no ordinary manner, 
saying, “ Great and in honor is the merciful man; but to find a 
faithful man is difficult.” If it is a great thing to find any one 
so ever merciful or faithful, how much greater an emperor whom 
power impels toward revenge, but whom, nevertheless, compassion 
recalls from taking vengeance? What is more illustrious than the 
faith of an emperor whom sovereignty does not exalt, pride does 
not elevate, but piety bows down? Of whom Solomon admirably 
says: “The threatening of an unjust king is like the roaring of 
a lion, but as dew upon the grass, so also is his cheerfulness.”

Therefore what a great thing it is to lay aside the terror of power, 
and prefer the sweetness of granting pardon? 

13. Theodosius of august memory thought he had received a 
kindness when asked to pardon, and he was more disposed to for- 
giveness at the time when the emotions of his wrath had been 
greatest. The token of forgiveness was that he had been angry, 
and what was feared in others was desired in him, that he be moved 
to wrath. It was the relief of the accused that although he had 
power over all, he preferred to expostulate as a father, rather than 
to punish as a judge. Often we have seen those whom he was re- 
buking, trembling and convicted of crime, and when they had 
despaired, we have seen them freed from the charge. For he wished 
to win them, not to punish as a judge of justice; he was not a 
dispenser of punishment, for he never denied pardon to one con- 
fessing his guilt; or if there was anything which the secret eon- 
science concealed, he reserved that for God. Men feared that voice 
of his more than punishment, because the emperor acted with such 
caution as to prefer to attach men to himself by conscientious 
motives rather than by fear. , 

14. It is said that the greatest of the philosophers granted im- 
munity from punishment to those crimes which had been com- 
mitted through anger, but the divine Scriptures say better, ‘** Be 
angry and sin not.” He preferred rather to lessen sin than to 
excuse it. It is better to find praise for mercy in an occasion for 
indignation than to be incited by wrath toward revenge. 

15. Who then will doubt that he will be a powerful protector 
for his sons in the House of God? By the favor of the Lord, the 
Emperor Arcadius is already a robust youth; Honorius knocks 
forthwith at the door of manhood, a little older than Josias. For 
the latter, deprived of a father, assumed the government and 
reigned continuously for thirty-one years, and he pleased the Lord 
because, better than the other kings of Israel, he celebrated the 
pasch of the Lord and abolished the errors of ceremonies. Asa 
likewise, though weak in health of body up to the time when he 
succeeded to the throne, reigned in Jerusalem forty years; he it 
was who, when hard pressed by an infinite and innumerable multi- 
tude of Ethiopians, trusted that by the aid of the Lord even among 
a few he could be saved. Would that he had been as faithful during 
his course as devout at its beginning! For, saved among a few

and victor, afterwards he left the Lord and asked aid from the 
Syrians and applied to physicians for a disease of the feet. When 
he had received so great indications of divine favour, he should 
not have abandoned his helper but retained Him. Therefore the 
physicians did not benefit him and as an unbeliever he paid the

16. But their fathers, Abiam and Amon, were both unbelievers. 
Theodosius, however, was full of the fear of God, was full of mercy, 
and we hope that he stands before Christ as a protector of his 
children, if the Lord be propitious to human affairs. The merciful 
man is praiseworthy who, while he assists others, is mindful of 
himself and with the remedy of another cures his own wounds. 
For he who knows how to forgive realizes that he is human, and 
he follows the way of Christ who, when he assumed flesh, chose 
to come into this world as a Redeemer rather than a Judge. 

17. Whence the psalmist beautifully has said: “I have loved, 
because the Lord will hear the voice of my prayer.” While this 
psalm was being read, we heard, as it were, Theodosius himself 
speaking. “I have loved” he says. I recognize his pious voice, 
and its testimonies also do I recognize. And he has truly loved 
who fulfilled his duty diligently, who spared his enemies, who loved 
his foes, who pardoned those by whom he was entreated, who did 
not even suffer those who strove for his power to perish. That 
voice is the saying of one not partially, but fully perfected in the 
Law. “I have loved for love is the fulfilling of the Law.” But 
let us hear what he has loved. When the kind of love is not men- 
tioned, certainly the grace of divine charity is signified whereby 
we love that which is to be desired above all things desirable, con- 
cerning which it is written: “ Thou shalt love the Lord thy God.” 

18. And so the pious soul departing from earth and filled with 
the Holy Spirit, when questioned, as it were, by those who hastened 
to meet it when it ascended to the high and supernal regions, kept 
saying, “I have loved.” Nothing fuller than this, nothing more 
definite. Angels and archangels were questioning, “ What hast 
thou done on earth?” For God alone is the witness of secret 
things. The soul kept saying, “I have loved,” that is to say, “I 
have fulfilled the Law, I have not neglected the Gospel”; that 
is to say, “I have offered myself to death, and all the day long 
I am counted as a sheep for the slaughter.” ‘ And therefore I

19. The Lord Jesus also teaches in the Gospel that this com- 
mandment of the Law must be observed, when he says to Peter: 
“Simon, son of John, lovest thou Me?” and he answered: “ Thou 
knowest, Lord, that I love Thee,” and He said a second time: 
“Simon, son of John, lovest thou Me?” and again he answered: 
“ Yea, Lord, Thou knowest that I love Thee,” and when asked a 
third time, he said: “ Lord, Thou knowest all things, Thou knowest 
that I love Thee.” His threefold answer confirmed his love, or 
effaced the fault of his threefold denial. And here if we seek, we 
shall find the threefold answer: “I have loved, because the Lord 
will hear the voice of my prayer.” I have loved since He has 
inclined his ear to me, that in my day I might call upon Him. 
I have loved because I have found tribulation and sorrow, and for 
the sake of my God, I have not fled the dangers of hell but have 
waited that they might seize and discover me.” 

20. He also says beautifully, “I have loved,” because now he 
had completed the course of this life, and so the Apostle also says 
when in the midst of his suffering, “I have fought a good fight, 
I have finished my course, I have kept the faith. As to the rest, 
there is laid up for me a crown of justice.” Great is the Lord 
who has given us the struggle, whereby he who has conquered 
merits to be crowned. “I have loved,” he says trustingly, “ because 
the Lord will hear the voice of my prayer.” 

21. “J have loved and therefore ‘ he has inclined his ear’ to me, 
to raise up the fallen, to quicken the dead.” For God does not 
incline his ear to hear corporally but to condescend to us, whereby 
He deigns to hear us and to lift up the substance of our weakness. 
He inclines himself toward us that our prayer may ascend to Him. 
He does not need a voice who offers mercy; for neither did He 
need a voice who heard Moses, though silent, and He kept saying 
that Moses cried out to Him, although he did not speak, but was 
interceding with unspeakable groanings. God also knew how to 
hear blood, for which no voice exists nor tongue is present; but 
he heard its voice through the glory of its sacred suffering. It 
cried out in martyrdom, it cried out in the parricide which it suf- 
fered on account of a sacrifice.

22. “I have loved,” he said, and “ for that reason loving I have 
done the will of the Lord, and I have invoked Him not on a few, 
but on all the days of my life.” For to invoke Him on certain 
days and not on all is the action of one who is haughty, not of 
one who hopes. It is likewise to return the debt of gratitude after 
the manner of those who abound in wealth not from a spirit of 
devotion. And so Paul said: “In all things give thanks.” For 
when do you not have something which you owe to God? Or when 
are you without a gift of God, you whose daily enjoyment of living 
is from God? “For what hast thou, which thou hast not re- 
ceived?” Therefore, because you always receive, always invoke; 
and since what you have is from God, always acknowledge that 
you are a debtor.. Still, I prefer that you pay your debts as a 
lover, than as one forced to do so. 

23. Do you hear him saying: “ ‘ The sorrows of death have com- 
passed me?’ Still, I have loved the Lord even in the sorrows of 
death. ‘The perils of hell have found me,’ not fearing indeed, 
but loving, but hoping, because no distress, no persecution, no 
dangers, no sword shall separate me from Christ.” Finally, he 
found tribulation and sorrow willingly knowing that “ tribulation 
worketh patience, patience trial, trial hope.” ‘Truly as a good 
athlete he sought the contest that he might gain the crown; which 
however, he knew had been given to him not through his own 
strength but by the aid of God; for he could not have been vic- 
torious unless he had called upon Him who helps those who are 
struggling. 

24. Miserable man enters the lists in order to be victorious and 
of his own accord runs headlong into danger, unless the name of 
the Lord be present with him, unless, when he fears, he prays, 
saying: “ O Lord deliver my soul.” Hence the following apostolic 
saying: “ But I see a law of my flesh fighting against the law of 
my mind, and captivating me in the law of sin, that is in my 
members. Unhappy man that I am, who shall deliver me from 
the body of this death? The grace of God, by Jesus Christ our 
Lord.” 

25, He is victorious, who hopes for the grace of God, not he who 
presumes upon his own strength. For why do you not presume 
grace, since you have a merciful One presiding over the contest. 
“For the Lord is merciful and just, and our God sheweth mercy.”

He set forth mercy twice, justice once. Justice is in the midst, 
inclosed by a double hedge of mercy; for sins superabound, there- 
fore let mercy superabound. With the Lord there is an abundance 
of all virtues, for He is the Lord of virtues. Still there is no 
justice without mercy, nor without the exercise of mercy is there 
justice, as it is written, “ Be not over-just.” That which is above 
measure, even if it be good, still one does not endure it. Preserve 
the measure that you may receive according to the measure. 

26. Still mercy has not impeded justice; for mercy is itself jus- 
tice. “He hath distributed, he hath given to the poor, his justice 
remaineth forever.” For the just man knows that he ought to 
succor the feeble and the needy. Hence the Lord, coming to bap- 
tism in order to pardon our sins who are weak, said to John: 
“Suffer it to be so now. For so it becometh us to fulfill all jus- 
tice.” Therefore it is evident that justice is mercy and mercy is 
justice. For if the mercy of God did not sustain us, how could 
we survive as infants, in the very first moment of existence when, 
borne from the womb from warmth into cold, from moisture into 
dryness, we are thrown forth like fishes which a flood of nature, 
as it were, had cast shipwrecked into this life. Reason is lacking, 
but divine grace does not fail. Therefore He Himself guards the 
little ones, or at least those who by an act of humility confess that 
they are little ones. 

2%. Good therefore is humility, which delivers those who are in 
danger and raises up the fallen. He knew it who said: “ Behold 
it is I, I have sinned, and I the shepherd have done wickedly ; and 
these in this flock what have they done? Let thy hand be against 
me.” Well he says this who made his kingdom subject to God and 
did penance and, having confessed his sin, asked pardon. He 
himself attained salvation through humility. Christ humbled Him- 
self to raise up all. He has attained the rest of Christ who has 
followed the humility of Christ. 

98. And so because Theodosius, the Emperor, showed him- 
self humble and, when sin had stolen upon him, asked pardon, 
his soul has turned to its rest, as the Scripture holds, which says: 
“Turn my soul unto thy rest, for the Lord hath been bountiful to 
thee.” Beautifully it says to the soul: “ Turn,” that the soul, as 
it were, tired out with the continued sweat of each work, may turn 
from labor to rest. The horse is turned toward the stable when

it has finished its course; the ship to its port, where it is drawn 
up to safe moorings from the force of the waves. But what does 
he mean by saying “to thy rest” unless you understand it ac- 
cording to the words of the Lord Jesus: “Come ye blessed of my 
Father, possess the kingdom prepared for you by inheritance from 
the foundation of the world?” For we receive, as it were, an 
inherited possession, the things which have been promised to us, 
for God is faithful, who does not withdraw what he has once pre- 
pared for His servants. If our faith endure, His promise like- 
wise endures. 

29. See O man the grace of Christ about you; as yet you are 
harassed on earth, but in heaven you possess. There then, let your 
heart be, where your possession is. This is the rest which is due 
the just, and is denied the unworthy. Whence says the Lord, “ As 
I swore in my wrath, that they shall not enter into my rest.” For 
he who has not known the ways of the Lord shall not enter into 
the rest of the Lord, but he who has fought the good fight and 
has finished his course, to him it is said: “Turn to thy rest.” It 
is a noble rest to pass by the things of the world and to find repose 
in that celestial fellowship of mysteries which are above the world. 
This is the rest toward which the prophet was eager to hasten, 
saying: “ Who will give me wings like a dove and I will fly and 
be at rest?” The holy man knows this rest of his; to this rest 
he says his soul must turn. Thus then was his soul in its rest, to 
which he says it must return. This is the rest of the great Sabbath, 
that each one of the saints is above the sensible things of the world 
wholly intent upon that intelligible mystery and keeping close to 
God. This is that rest of the Sabbath on which God rested from 
all the works of this world. 

30. Theodosius, now at peace, rejoices that he has been snatched 
away from the cares of this world and lifts up his soul and directs 
it to that perpetual rest, declaring that he has been well cared for, 
inasmuch as God has snatched his soul from death, which death 
he frequently withstood in_this hazardous state of the world, dis- 
turbed by the waves of sin. He has also snatched his eyes from 
tears, for sorrow and sadness and mourning shall flee away. And 
elsewhere we have: “ He shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; 
and death shall be no more, nor mourning, nor crying, nor sorrow.” 
If then death shall be no more, he will not be able to experience

a fall, when he is in that rest, but he will please God in the land 
of the living. For as man is here enveloped in a body of death 
subject to falls and transgressions, not so indeed there. And so, 
that is the land of the living where the soul is, which has been

31. Into this rest Theodosius hastened to enter, that is, to go 
into the city of Jerusalem, concerning which it has been said: 
““ And the kings of the earth shall bring their glory into it.” That 
is true glory which is there assumed; that the most blessed king- 
dom which is there possessed, to which the Apostle was hastening, 
saying: “We are confident, then, and agree to be absent rather 
from the body and to be present with the Lord; and therefore we 
strive, whether absent or present to please Him.” 

32. And so freed from a doubtful struggle, Theodosius of au- 
gust memory now enjoys perpetual light and lasting tranquility, 
and in exchange for all that he did in this body, he rejoices in 
the fruits of a divine reward. Therefore because Theodosius of 
august memory loved the Lord his God, he has merited the com- 
panionship of the saints. 

33. And to conclude my discourse by a sort of peroration—I have 
loved the merciful man, humble in power and endowed with a pure 
heart and a gentle disposition, such a one as God is accustomed 
to love saying: “ Above whom shall I rest, unless above the humble 
and gentle?” 

34, I have loved the man who esteemed an admonitor more than 
a flatterer. He threw on the ground all the royal attire which he 
was wearing; he wept publicly in the Church, for his sin which 
had stolen upon him by the deceit of others; he prayed for pardon 
with groans and with tears. That which brings a blush to private 
citizens, the Emperor did not blush to do, that is, to perform pen- 
ance publicly; nor did a day pass afterwards on which he did not 
grieve for that mistake of his. What of the fact that when he had 
gained an illustrious victory, still because the enemy had fallen 
in battle he abstained from a participation of the sacraments, until 
he experienced the grace of God around him by the arrival of his 
children ?

36. “The sorrows of death have compassed me; the perils of hell 
have found me”; for the dangers are of the many, but the remedies 
of the few. The bishop is imperilled in all circumstances; he is 
distressed among all the guilty; for what others suffer, he himself 
endures; and in turn he is freed, when others who are bound are 
freed from danger. I am crushed at heart because a man has been 
taken away whom we can scarcely replace. But still, O Lord, Thou 
alone shouldst be supplicated ; Thou shouldst be implored to repro- 
duce him in his sons. Thou, Lord, the keeper also of little ones 
in this lowliness, “ save those hoping in Thee.” Give perfect rest to 
Thy servant Theodosius, that rest which Thou hast prepared for 
Thy saints. Let his soul turn there, whence it descended, where 
he cannot feel the sting of death, where he knows that this death 
is not the end of nature but of error. For in that he died, he died 
to sin; so that now there can be no place for sin; but he will rise 
again in order that his life may be restored more perfectly by 
renewed gifts. 

37. I have loved and so I accompany him to the land of 
the living, and I will not abandon him, until, by my tears and 
prayers I shall lead the man whither his merits summon, unto the 
holy mountain of God; where there is eternal life; where there is 
no corruption, no sickness, no mourning, no sorrow, no companion- 
ship of the dead; a true land of the living where “ this mortal shall 
put on immortality and this corruptible shall put on incorruption.” 
A noble repose which, satisfies the prayer of the loving, a most 
glorious promise. Therefore the Hundred and Fourteenth Psalm, 
Alleluia, is written. Finally, in the Fourteenth Psalm, we have 
already learned the perfection of man. But made in this world, 
man may be perfect, but still subject to sin, because he is living in 
time; in the other world there is true perfection where indeed sin 
has ceased, and the grace of perpetual rest has shone forth.

50. But I ask: Why is the holy relic upon the bridle, unless to
